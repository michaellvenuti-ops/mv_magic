Key Number Middles for NBA and College Basketball

Computed from ESPN final scores — NBA: 2,644 games, 2023-24 + 2024-25 (regular season, play-in, playoffs) · College (NCAAB, Division I): 6,292 games, 2024-25 · Built Nov 10, 2026 · Football companion: NFL Key Numbers
The headline: basketball has no true key numbers. Football's margin of 3 lands in 14.6% of NFL games — a spike you can build a scan around. Basketball scoring (1s, 2s and 3s over ~100 possessions) smooths everything out: the most common NBA margin lands just 5.67% of the time, the most common college margin 5.93%, and the most common totals land barely 2.5% of the time. There are no keys — there are clusters. A basketball middle's value comes from the width of its window over the cluster, not from touching one magic number. The scan built on this page (Basketball Key-Number Middles, on the PC) therefore scores every middle by its modeled both-win probability instead of checking exact keys.

NBA — victory margins

Gold = the plateau core (each lands ~5-5.7%). Margins 2-9 together cover 42.2% of all games; every margin from 1 to 12 lands between 4.2% and 5.7% — a plateau, not a peak. The top eight margins are within sampling noise of each other. Margins of 20+ still happen in 20.4% of games.

NBA — final totals

A smooth bell centered at 227.0. The single most common total, 223, lands just 2.46% of the time; the working range 215-240 covers 48.0% of games. No key totals exist — a totals middle is buying a range, never a number. Totals land odd slightly more often than even (50.5% odd) — there is no even-number clustering to exploit.

College (NCAAB) — victory margins

Gold = the core cluster 2-5 (23.0% of games combined; each of 2, 3, 4, 5 lands 5.5-5.9%, effectively tied). Blue = the secondary shelf 7-9. Margins 2-9 cover 41.5% of games. College has more blowouts than the NBA — margins of 20+ in 24.8% of games (mismatches, including D1-vs-non-D1 games in the sample).

College (NCAAB) — final totals

A smooth bell centered at 146.3. Most common total 139 at 2.56%; the range 135-160 covers 52.6% of games. Same story as the NBA: no key totals, and odd totals are marginally more common than even (51.4%).

What the scan does with this

The Basketball Key-Number Middles scan (PC, every 30 minutes, DarkHorse middles board) reads every NBA and college middle and scores it from the distributions above: both-win numbers at full weight, whole-number endpoints at half weight (one leg wins, the other pushes). It banks and alerts only:
  • Spread middles with a modeled both-win probability of 10% or more — roughly two cluster margins of window, e.g. an NBA middle covering margins 6, 7 and 8 models at ~16.8%.
  • Total middles with a modeled both-win probability of 8% or more — roughly four totals of window near the center of the bell.
Both legs must be -170 or better (clean pairs, both -140 or better, are flagged), same discipline as the football scans. Plus a SPANKY tier (wired in Nov 10, 2026): college rows that meet his Ep 14/39 baselines — spread windows of 1.5+ points or total windows of 2.5+ points with both legs -125 or better — are banked and alerted labeled SPANKY even when their modeled probability sits under the bars above. His 2.0-point total window is break-even, not +EV, so it does not qualify; and he gave no NBA width rule, so NBA rows run on the modeled tier only. Live results: Basketball Key-Number Middles board. The modeled percentages are estimates from the historical landing rates — a guide to which windows are worth money, not a promise about any single game.

What Spanky said (Be Better Bettors)

Across the full 93-episode catalog, Spanky never names a key number for basketball — no NBA or college margin or total gets the treatment he gave the NFL's 3 and 7 (and totals 37/41) in Episode 2. His basketball middling math is uniform-value math instead, which is exactly what a smooth distribution implies:
  • Ep 14 — Twitter Questions (Mar 6, 2020): on college basketball totals, you need about 2.5 points of line differential for a +EV middle; 2 points is roughly break-even.
  • Ep 39 — Twitter Questions (Dec 14, 2020): half-point values — in the NFL every number has its own value, but in college basketball a 1.5-point side middle and a 2-point total middle are the baselines at -110, and a half point on a college total is worth about 5 cents, roughly 2.5%.
  • Ep 93 — Twitter Q+A (Nov 26, 2025): in the NBA, a half point on spreads is worth about $0.78 — one flat figure for every number, quoted in his green-zone/red-zone value model.
  • Guest Elihu Feustel's conversion charts put NBA total push rates at ~2.15% for high totals vs ~2.45% for very low ones — push probability drifts mildly with the level of the total, but no single total stands out as a key.
Cross-check against the data on this page: Spanky's ~2.5% per half point on college totals matches the measured ~2.1-2.6% landing rate per total near the center almost exactly, and his 2-point break-even / 2.5-point +EV rule for college total middles is the same math the scan's modeled probabilities produce. Where the scan is stricter: its 8% modeled bar for totals (at -110 both ways) sits a little above his baseline — plus-money legs, which many real pairs carry, lower the probability a middle actually needs. On sides, his 1.5-point college baseline is a width rule, not a number rule — again, the cluster idea, not keys.

Cross-checks & caveats

Published research agrees with the computed data: WSN's multi-year NBA table also puts margin 7 on top (~6.6%) with 5, 6 and 8 next, and concludes NBA key numbers are far weaker than the NFL's; Boyd's Bets (every college game since 2006) and BettorGame (52,018 D1 games) both put college margin 3 on top at ~6.2-6.3% with 2, 5, 4 and 7 next — the same cluster found here (in this single-season sample, 5 and 4 edge out 3, well within noise). Every source concludes totals have no key numbers. Caveats: the college sample includes D1 teams' games against non-D1 opponents and all postseason tournaments, which fattens the blowout tail; overtime games count at their final scores, matching how bets settle; one college season and two NBA seasons — ranks inside each cluster would shuffle a little with more data, the flatness would not.
