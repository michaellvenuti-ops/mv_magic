The big idea

A financial plan tailored to your situation can reduce your tax bill — but taxes should never drive the investment strategy.

Fisher's point, in the reel and in the free guide it promotes: as wealth grows, so does the complexity of the tax bill. A tailored plan — tax-aware investing, estate and legacy planning, philanthropy, Social Security and healthcare — can keep more of your wealth working for you. The guide is written for investors with $5 million or more, and Fisher describes itself as a fiduciary money manager overseeing $387 billion globally (as of March 31, 2026).

  • Tax-aware portfolio management — tax-efficient investing, tax-reduction strategies, capital-gains evaluations and business tax planning, backed by a comprehensive tax analysis and tax-return review.

The tax rates that matter

Everything below flows from one fact: not all investment income is taxed the same way.

Income typeFederal rate
Qualified dividends & long-term capital gains (held > 1 year)0% / 15% / 20%, depending on income
Ordinary income (bond interest, REIT payouts, short-term gains)Up to 37%
Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) — on investment income above $200,000 single / $250,000 married+3.8%
Top effective federal rate on long-term gains (20% + 3.8% NIIT)23.8%
Top effective federal rate on short-term gains (37% + 3.8% NIIT)40.8%

That gap — 23.8% vs 40.8% at the top — is why holding period and account placement matter so much. Waiting one extra day past the one-year mark can nearly halve the tax on a gain. Sources: Quantix Invest — Tax-Efficient Investing; AE Tax Advisors.

Asset location: the right investment in the right account

Asset allocation is what you own. Asset location is which account each piece lives in — and it can cut a portfolio's tax drag without changing the overall mix. The rule of thumb, confirmed across several sources:

AccountTax treatmentBest suited for
Taxable brokerageDividends and realized gains taxed yearly; step-up in basis at death; foreign tax credit availableBroad stock index funds and ETFs, international stock funds, municipal bonds, buy-and-hold individual stocks
Tax-deferred (traditional 401(k) / IRA)Everything taxed as ordinary income on withdrawalTaxable bonds, REITs, TIPS, high-yield bonds — anything spinning off ordinary income
Roth (Roth IRA / Roth 401(k))Never taxed againHighest expected-return assets — stock funds, especially aggressive tilts

Two details worth knowing: don't hold municipal bonds inside an IRA or 401(k) — you'd accept munis' lower yield for a tax exemption the account already provides. And don't let location distort allocation — decide the stock/bond mix first, then place assets. One estimate puts location optimization at roughly 0.1–0.3% per year for a typical multi-account investor: not life-changing alone, but free money for a one-time setup decision.

Sources: New York Life — Tax-Efficient Investing Strategies for High Earners; Quantix Invest.

Tax-loss harvesting — and splitting gains across years

Tax-loss harvesting: sell a security that has declined, use the loss to offset capital gains dollar-for-dollar. If losses exceed gains, up to $3,000 a year can offset ordinary income, with the excess carried forward indefinitely. The catch is the wash-sale rule: if you buy the same or a substantially identical investment within 30 days before or after the sale, the IRS disallows the loss. The standard move is to buy a similar-but-not-identical replacement to keep your market exposure, then reconsider the original after 30 days.

Splitting gains across tax years: concentrated positions — from equity compensation or one stock's long run — can be sold in pieces across year-end boundaries (part in late 2025, part in 2026, part in early 2027) to keep each year's gain in a lower bracket. Fisher's article uses exactly that example.

Direct indexing: instead of owning an index fund, own the individual stocks directly, which lets you harvest losses at the security level all year. One source claims it can add 1–2% a year in after-tax return versus a plain index fund — it shines when the account is large enough and the time horizon long enough to justify the extra complexity.

Sources: Fisher Investments — Don't Let the Tax Tail Wag the Portfolio; New York Life; AllCarJ — Stock Market Guide for High Net-Worth Investors.

Municipal bonds: tax-free income

Municipal bond interest is exempt from federal tax, and typically exempt from state and local tax when the bonds are issued by your home state. For an investor in the top bracket, a muni's after-tax yield can beat a taxable bond's — always compare on a tax-equivalent yield basis, and weigh the issuer's credit risk. Municipal bonds belong in taxable accounts, not in IRAs.

Sources: AE Tax Advisors; New York Life.

Concentrated positions: the exchange fund

A single stock that has run for years can leave you with a huge embedded gain — selling means a big tax bill, holding means concentration risk. An exchange fund (not an ETF) lets you swap the concentrated position for a diversified pool of stocks, keeping the same cost basis and deferring capital gains until you sell. The price: you must hold the fund for seven years or face penalties for early redemption, and you don't fully control which securities you diversify into. Fisher calls it "less a tax reduction tool than a delay-and-diversify tool."

Source: Fisher Investments — Don't Let the Tax Tail Wag the Portfolio.

Traditional vs. Roth — and the order you draw from

Both traditional and Roth IRAs let assets grow tax-free, but they tax the money differently: traditional contributions are pre-tax (often deductible now) and withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income; Roth contributions are post-tax and withdrawals — principal, interest and gains — are tax-free.

  • Required minimum distributions: traditional IRAs force withdrawals starting at age 73 (75 starting in 2033). Roth IRAs have no RMDs during the owner's life.
  • Withdrawal sequencing: drawing from the traditional IRA first can shrink future RMDs (which would otherwise push taxable income up). Drawing from the taxable account first lets tax-advantaged accounts keep growing. Which is right depends on whether you expect a higher or lower tax rate in the future than today.
  • Roth conversions: converting a traditional IRA to Roth means paying income tax on the converted amount now — and the added income can push you into a higher bracket for that portion. The calculus is personal; a Roth 401(k) option can change the answer.
  • Low-income years are an opening: in an unusually low-income year, harvesting gains up to the 0% long-term capital gains bracket resets cost basis at zero federal tax.

Sources: Fisher Investments; Buckeye Wealth Partners / Altruist — Make Your Portfolio More Tax-Efficient.

Philanthropy & legacy: give smart, not just generously

  • Qualified charitable distributions (QCDs): if you're required to take RMDs, you can direct some or all of the RMD to charity. A QCD satisfies the RMD and excludes the donated amount from gross income — so the RMD doesn't boost your income. The 2025 annual maximum is $108,000 per individual ($216,000 for married couples filing jointly).
  • Gifting securities: donating appreciated stock instead of cash avoids the capital gain and still earns the deduction — one of the tools Fisher lists alongside charitable trusts, foundations and donor-advised funds.
  • Annual gift exclusion: for 2026, you can give up to $19,000 per recipient per year without using any lifetime exemption (per Fisher's guide).
  • Legacy planning: Fisher's guide stresses involving beneficiaries at the right time and making sure the estate plan — assets, business interests, charitable giving — actually reflects your wishes and informs how the money is managed.

Sources: Fisher Investments; Fisher Investments guide.

The warning: don't let the tax tail wag the portfolio

Fisher's own article is blunt about the limits: "Taxes are one consideration affecting personal finances, but they shouldn't drive your investment strategy." Buying a much more volatile investment purely for its tax treatment, or giving up liquidity and long-term returns to dodge a tax bill, can cost more than the tax itself. Their example: UK AIM shares bought for tax advantages in ISAs and pensions — exempt from some capital gains, dividend and inheritance taxes, but far more volatile, with a history of scandals and failures; the FTSE AIM All Share trailed the FTSE All Share by 46% to 301% since 2000. "Remember why you are investing: to reach your investment goals, not to dodge Uncle Sam (who will always take his bite)."

Source: Fisher Investments — Don't Let the Tax Tail Wag the Portfolio (Oct 7, 2025).

Quick checklist

  • Put tax-inefficient assets (bonds, REITs, high-turnover funds) in tax-deferred accounts; tax-efficient ones (index funds, ETFs, munis) in taxable; highest-growth in Roth.
  • Harvest losses to offset gains — and mind the 30-day wash-sale window.
  • Split big concentrated-position sales across tax years.
  • Compare munis to taxable bonds on a tax-equivalent yield basis.
  • Plan RMDs before age 73; consider QCDs if you're charitably inclined.
  • Use low-income years to harvest gains at the 0% bracket.
  • Review the plan with your tax professional — everyone's situation is different.
